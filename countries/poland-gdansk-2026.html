Gdańsk — 2026 Trip Notes

Gdańsk with Tracey

22nd – 25th February 2026
A snowy February escape filled with history, Baltic charm, and unforgettable food.

Album

Introduction: The Journey Begins

There's something special about a trip that starts before the sun is up. At 5:30am on Sunday 22nd February, Rhys dropped us at Stansted Airport, the world still dark and quiet around us. Tracey had been awake since 3am—I at least managed 4am. Flight FR545 with Ryanair would carry us eastward, away from the familiar and toward a city we'd chosen for reasons we couldn't quite articulate: cobbled streets, Baltic air, amber in shop windows, and the pull of a place we'd only ever seen in photographs.

Three days later, on Wednesday 25th at 7pm, Rhys would be there again, waiting to collect us and our bags full of memories.

But in between? That was ours to discover.

We were staying right in the heart of it all—Hotel Fama Residence on Dluga 81/83, Old Town. A base camp in the middle of the story, waiting to unfold.

Chapter 1: The Flight

This was our first time flying with Ryanair, and we'd done our homework. Chloe had prepared us well—the baggage rules are strict, the size checks are real, and the stories... well, everyone has a story about budget airlines. We packed carefully, measured everything twice, and braced ourselves for a rough ride.

What we got instead was a pleasant surprise. The check-in process was seamless, all digital on our phones. Security at Stansted, despite the early morning crowds, was polite and efficient. And then came the unexpected luck of the draw: I found myself with an entire row to myself, stretching out like I'd booked business class. Tracey was just behind me, an aisle seat with an empty space beside her. For a budget flight, it felt almost luxurious.

We settled in, exchanged a grin across the seats, and watched England disappear beneath the clouds.

The flight was barely an hour and forty minutes—a blink, really.

Chapter 2: Arrival

Being near the front of the plane meant we were off quickly and through to security almost before the queue had time to form. There were new regulations for Brits now: photos and fingerprints. It sounded ominous, but it was over in moments, efficient and matter-of-fact.

Then came the race for connectivity. I fumbled with data roaming; Tracey had been smarter, loading up an e-SIM for Poland before we left. She won. Within minutes, she'd booked a taxi. By the time we walked out of arrivals, it was already waiting for us. Clean, efficient, and ready to go.

Our neighbours, Tracey and Pete, had done this journey just a few weeks earlier. They'd warned us about the cold, so we came prepared like we were heading for an Arctic expedition. Layers upon layers. Coats, gloves, scarves, hats. And my secret weapon—USB heated hand warmers, charged and ready.

We felt smugly prepared.

Then we stepped outside. It was only -3°C. It wasn't the temperature that surprised us—we'd expected that. It was the way the cold air made everything feel clean and sharp. The snow on the roads didn't look slushy or grey; it looked fresh, like it had just fallen. We'd seen it from the air, of course—the whole city spread out below us, dusted in winter white—but feeling it underfoot was different. Real.

We didn't head straight to the hotel. The taxi took us directly to the Second World War Museum. No point wasting daylight.

Chapter 3: The Second World War Museum

The building itself was drab. It didn't have a striking presence from the outside, nothing that prepared you for what was inside. But once through the doors, the space opened up—large, airy, with staff everywhere ready to help direct us.

Tickets were sold on floor minus three. I spotted the automated machines and headed straight for them, but Tracey, ever more observant, pointed out the queue. We joined it, moved through quickly, and I suggested we opt for audio guides.

Best decision of the day.

The woman's voice on that guide—perfect tone, perfect speed, perfectly judged information—accompanied us through every room. She became, in a way, our companion for the next few hours.

Before entering, we noticed a cloakroom. The same setup at every museum we'd visit, and it made perfect sense: everyone arrives wrapped for winter, but nobody wants to carry coats and scarves through history. Signs directed us to lockers for backpacks, but we had no coins. We stood there, looking lost, until a female staff member approached and wordlessly handed us a plastic token. A small kindness we still remember.

Then we walked in.

The audio guide directed us through each room, explaining the displays, the timeline, the impact of other nations on the war. Screens showed films from each era, and the guide let us listen in English, resetting easily for the next. We moved methodically, both of us impressed by the information, the layout, the sheer scale of it all.

But it was the end that stayed with me.

Standing there, surrounded by the weight of it all, my mind kept circling back to one thought: how much horror happened. How a small number of people were able to control whole nations. And how this same playbook has repeated itself in countless other wars—and is still being used today, playing out in current news as I write this.

I almost wanted to write something. A parody, maybe, in the style of Armando Iannucci. Something to help people see how toxic leaders influence us all, how easily it happens, how we're not as immune as we like to think.

The museum didn't just inform me. It provoked me.

And I suspect that's exactly what it was meant to do.

Chapter 4: First Steps in the Old Town

From the museum, we walked to our hotel—about fifteen minutes through streets dusted with snow. Some paths had been gritted, others hadn't, so we picked our way carefully, ginger steps on uncertain ground.

Hotel Fama Residence welcomed us without fuss. Check-in was smooth until the receptionist mentioned the foreigner hotel tax—20.26 ZLT, payable in cash only. A government scheme, she explained, common across the globe now. But cash only, at the first hurdle, caught us off guard.

Fortunate, then, that our neighbours had given us some cash before we left. Otherwise we'd have been hunting for an ATM, paying fees, grumbling. We handed over 30 ZLT. The receptionist searched for change, searched some more, then shrugged and waved away the 0.26 with a smile.

A small victory to start the trip.

We dumped our bags, barely paused, and headed straight back out. The Old Town was waiting.

Chapter 5: The First Evening

We walked. Took photos of everything. Checked out restaurants and shops, getting our bearings, letting the city introduce itself.

Then a man appeared. He came bustling out of a local Polish restaurant, cheerful and animated, and started recommending dishes to us with genuine enthusiasm. We liked him immediately. We liked his recommendations too. But the place was full, he explained—maybe come back a little later?

That worked perfectly for us. We're both always eager to eat locally, to avoid international chains and generic food. (Though we'd noticed, with some bemusement, just how many kebab shops and Italian pizza places seemed to have taken root here.)

So we wandered on and found a bar by the river. Settled in. Ordered a beer and a plate of pierogi to share—neither of us had tried them before. The service was slow, painfully slow, but we gave it a chance. The pierogi were... OK. Fine. Enough to keep us going, because we had plans.

More wandering. More photos. At around 6pm, we returned.

The restaurant was nice, the staff friendly. Tracey ordered beef stroganoff; I had pork goulash. Both dishes were, in hindsight, OK. A little bland. But we were hungry, and the beer was cold, and I persuaded Tracey to order a carafe of wine.

After eating, we walked again. The streets and buildings were lit up beautifully against the night sky. We took more photos—too many, probably—then finally surrendered to exhaustion and headed back to the hotel.

We'd been up since 4am. Tracey since 3am. Sleep came easily.

Chapter 6: Noisy Nights & Painful Shoulders

We'd been warned the street could be loud overnight.

It was.

Tracey, closer to the window, bore the brunt of it. At 2:30am, other guests decided to hold a conversation right outside our door. Sleep, when it came, was fractured.

But morning arrived anyway, and with it, Tracey's pinched nerve. Her shoulder was agony. She said nothing much, just soldiered on, because what else is there to do?

We dressed, layered up, and headed out to find breakfast.

Chapter 7: The Bakery

We found a Georgian bakery not far from the hotel. Coffee for me, Earl Grey for Tracey. Food: a filled croissant, a doughnut for Tracey, and for me, bread covered in cheese.

The two staff members behind the counter were... miserable. Stone-faced. Efficient but utterly joyless. We noted it, shrugged, and ate our breakfast anyway.

This detail will become important later.

Chapter 8: Leon the Collie Dog

We wanted to go to Sopot. I'd done my research, found the train station close to us, figured out the ticket machine, located the right platform. We settled into our seats, pleased with ourselves.

A few stops later, a lady boarded with a dog. A Collie. The friendliest collie you've ever seen. His name was Leon, and he made a beeline straight for us. Snuggled up for pets. Lay down at our feet. The lady laughed and said we should change ownership.

We very nearly considered it.

Chapter 9: Sopot in the Snow

Sopot was snow-covered and beautiful. We walked past the Crooked House, past bakeries, toward the beach. (Every public toilet, we discovered, required payment. A theme.)

Then we stepped onto the beach itself—snow-covered sand, untouched in places, leading down to the Baltic Sea. By the water's edge, locals were feeding swans and smaller birds, the creatures gathered in surprising numbers.

We walked. We took more photos. We breathed in air that felt like it had travelled straight from the Arctic.

Eventually, we needed warming up. A bakery appeared like a mirage: two smiley, attentive ladies inside. Coffee for me, hot chocolate with a bun for Tracey. We sat, thawed, and smiled back at them.

Chapter 10: The Best Pierogi of Our Lives

On the train back toward Gdansk, I had an idea. A restaurant I'd spotted in my research: Pieroginia Mandu. Instead of getting off at our usual stop, we got off one stop early and walked.

We were greeted warmly, seated immediately. I ordered a beer; Tracey ordered tea. When the tea arrived, it came in a cast iron pot with a base, a tray for the leaves, and a timer. The waitress explained: the timer would go off when it had steeped to perfection.

Rhys had taught us the value of this way of drinking tea years ago. We exchanged a glance—impressed.

Then the pierogi arrived. And the waitress had recommended sauces to suit each order. And they were, without exaggeration, unbelievably delicious.

We ate slowly, savouring every bite. This, we thought, is what we came for.

Chapter 11: Solidarity & Second Chances

We walked back to the Old Town as evening fell, our path taking us past the European Solidarity Centre. We went in, walked through. It was interesting, certainly, but less powerful than the WWII museum. The ending, though, was effective: the exhibit focused on the three crosses statue outside, visible through the windows, tying the indoor story to the outdoor monument.

As darkness settled properly, we found ourselves by the riverside again, walking along the bars, looking for a place called Whiskey in a Jar. We walked in circles first—of course—then finally found it.

The entrance was poor. The seating was poor. We walked straight back out.

Crossing the bridge, Tracey spotted another place. Let's try here, she said.

We walked in. Amazing reception. Attentive staff. The waiter asked if we wanted drinks, and I asked which vodka he'd recommend. He showed us a filtered local one: Sedina Filtered Wheat, from the Spirit of Polish Distillery. Tracey stuck with beer.

We both ordered Zurek—the local sour rye soup. It was amazing. Perfect portion. We lingered, chatted pleasantly with the staff, and left feeling that the evening had redeemed itself entirely.

Chapter 12: Westerplatte

Chapter 12: Westerplatte

Tuesday morning. Our last full day. We wanted Westerplatte.

I found the bus, figured out the route, and we boarded for the 45-minute journey. On the bus with us: locals, English guests, families. All of us heading to the same place.

We all got off together. And we all stood there, looking around in confusion.

The museum building was large, rust-coloured, industrial. But where was the monument? I checked my phone and suggested we walk along the coast, toward where I thought it might be.

The cold here was intense. All layers on, hoods up, gloves on. We trudged through snow-covered grass, looking for a path that didn't seem to exist, sinking occasionally into deep crevasses. Thank God for waterproof shoes.

Others were heading the same way, so we followed. Eventually, a proper pathway emerged—though signs were notably absent.

The path led past a bombed building, then opened up to reveal the tall monolith on its mound. We climbed to the top, took photos in every direction. Across the river, a lighthouse stood watch.

We walked back to the rust-coloured building, expecting more museum. It turned out to be just a staff help point. But there was a top floor with a view, and a café. We sat down with coffee and tea, waiting for the bus, looking out over the place where the first shots of the Second World War were fired.

It felt significant. And also oddly ordinary. Perhaps that's the point.

Chapter 13: Milk Bar

Back in the Old Town, we found a Milk Bar. A local concept—cheap, quick, tasty food, relics of the communist era preserved in form if not in politics.

Tracey ordered goulash. I ordered Bigos.

[For those who don't know: Bigos is traditional Polish hunter's stew. Sauerkraut, fresh cabbage, various meats, mushrooms. Smoky, tangy, savoury. The perfect winter dish.]

We loved it. Loved the concept, loved the food, loved the no-fuss authenticity. We vowed to return before we left.

(We didn't. But the vow was sincere.)

Chapter 14: Regrets & Nightcaps

Afternoon siesta. Essential.

We woke, walked to St Mary's Basilica, and found a brilliant craft brew place nearby. Stout for me, pale ale for Tracey. Strong beers, good vibes.

Then dinner. We headed to the riverside, and somehow—inexplicably—ended up at Billy's American Restaurant. Tracey had steak. I had a pulled beef burger.

Both dishes were OK. Fine. Edible.

We immediately regretted not eating local.

That night, we had one last drink at the hotel bar, then bed.

Chapter 15: Bakery Redemption & Amber Disappointment

Our final morning. We walked to the Amber Museum, arriving at 9:45. It didn't open until 10.

So we went back to the Georgian bakery. The one with the miserable women.

She was not miserable. She was smiley, chatty, delightful. We blinked, confused but pleased. Perhaps we'd caught her on a bad morning the first time. Perhaps we'd misjudged her entirely. Either way, we left with warm pastries and warm feelings.

The Amber Museum was disappointing. More of a sales shop than a museum. It killed an hour.

We wandered. Tried a few bars. Then, for reasons I can no longer explain, we decided to give the first bar—the one with the slow service on Day 1—a second chance.

It was still terrible. We walked out again.

Chapter 16: The Perfect Ending

We returned to the Bower bar. The place where we'd had Zurek on Monday night.

Tracey ordered Zurek again. I ordered ribs. The waiter beamed at our choices.

When he brought the dishes, he set Tracey's down with a flourish: "Enjoy the best dish of the house."

Then he placed my ribs in front of me: "Good luck. The size is huge."

He noticed my glass was nearly empty. Paused. Examined it with mock concern.

"There seems to be something wrong with your glass," he announced. "Let me get you another."

He returned with a full beer.

Before bringing our drinks initially, he'd asked where we were from. When we told him, he smiled, nodded, and said:

"Enjoy your British breakfast beer."

We laughed. We ate. We soaked in the warmth of a place that had, across two visits, become a highlight. A reminder that travel isn't just about sights and museums—it's about the people who make you feel welcome in a strange city.

Chapter 17: Home

At the airport, staff checked everyone's luggage against the size requirements. Some passengers were repacking. Some were paying extra.

We walked straight through.

Chloe's preparation had paid off.

On the plane, I'd been assigned a window seat at the back. Tracey had a middle seat at the front. I swapped with her—she needed the extra room at the window for her big coat.

The flight passed quickly. At Stansted, we boarded the shuttle bus. It stopped. A lady and her son got off, and we followed, but everyone else called out: "Not this stop!" Confused, we got back on. The bus continued to the very last stop—which, thankfully, was quick and easy.

At 7pm, Rhys was there. Waiting. Just as he'd promised.

EPILOGUE: DO VIRGINIA
---------------------

A few small things we learned:

Good morning = jean dobray
Thank you = JenKuya
Goodbye = do virginia

(We're aware the spelling is phonetic. It worked for us.)

Everyone spoke English. Everyone. One girl at a bakery said she couldn't, then took our entire order in English and replied to every question. We smiled and didn't mention it.


REFLECTION
----------

The WWII museum stayed with me.

How much horror happened. How a small number of people controlled whole nations. How the same playbook repeats, over and over, across wars and decades, still playing out in today's news.

I almost wanted to write something. A parody, maybe. In the style of Armando Iannucci. Something to help people see how toxic leaders influence us all, how easily we're led, how fragile democracy can be when no one's paying attention.

But maybe that's a project for another day.

For now, I have photos. I have memories. I have a story of three days in Gdansk with Tracey—the snow, the soup, the dog, the waiter who called beer "British breakfast."

And I have this book.

Which, I suppose, is exactly what I wanted.
